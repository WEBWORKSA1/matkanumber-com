## Two ways to group the same digits

The international system puts a comma after every three digits. The Indian system puts one after the first three digits from the right and then after every two.

| Value | Indian style | Name | International style | Name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10<sup>3</sup> | 1,000 | one thousand | 1,000 | one thousand |
| 10<sup>5</sup> | 1,00,000 | one lakh | 100,000 | one hundred thousand |
| 10<sup>6</sup> | 10,00,000 | ten lakh | 1,000,000 | one million |
| 10<sup>7</sup> | 1,00,00,000 | one crore | 10,000,000 | ten million |
| 10<sup>9</sup> | 1,00,00,00,000 | one hundred crore | 1,000,000,000 | one billion |

## Quick conversions

- 1 million = 10 lakh
- 1 crore = 10 million
- 1 billion = 100 crore
- 1 lakh crore = 1 trillion

A handy rule: to turn crore into million, multiply by 10. To turn lakh into million, divide by 10.

## Reading a number aloud

Take 4,56,78,912. Read each group with its name: four crore, fifty-six lakh, seventy-eight thousand, nine hundred twelve. The [number to words converter]({{ '/tools/number-to-words.html' | relative_url }}) does this for any number up to fifteen digits and shows both systems side by side.

## India's gift: place value and zero

The digits the whole world uses, 0 to 9 in a positional system, developed in India. The mathematician Brahmagupta set out rules for arithmetic with zero in 628 CE. The system travelled west through the Arabic-speaking world, which is why Europeans called the digits "Arabic numerals".

Place value is the reason arithmetic on paper is easy. Compare multiplying 48 by 12 with multiplying XLVIII by XII and you will see what the idea changed.

## Hindi number words

Hindi has a separate word for every number from 0 to 99 rather than building them from tens and units, so "forty-seven" is *saintalis* and "ninety-nine" is *ninyanave*. Every page in our [number library]({{ '/numbers/' | relative_url }}) lists the Hindi name.
