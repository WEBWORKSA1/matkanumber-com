## A pot first, a game later

In Hindi and several other Indian languages, a *matka* is a round earthen pot. Potters have thrown them for centuries, and households still use them to keep drinking water cool: water seeps through the porous clay and evaporates, which pulls heat out of the pot.

A pot with a narrow mouth is also a simple, honest randomiser. Drop in folded chits, shake, and let someone reach in without looking. Villages, schools and committees have picked names this way for generations.

## How the pot got attached to numbers

In mid-twentieth-century Bombay, traders bet on the opening and closing rates of cotton sent by teleprinter from the New York Cotton Exchange. That practice ended in 1961. Organisers needed a new source of numbers, and one answer was to write the digits 0 to 9 on slips, put them in a matka and draw. Playing cards were used later, but the name of the pot stayed.

Kalyanji Bhagat started the Worli draw in 1962 and Ratan Khatri launched a rival version in 1964. At its height in the 1980s and 1990s the trade reportedly turned over hundreds of crores of rupees a month. Police action and competition from other forms of gambling then shrank it sharply. Khatri died in 2020, aged 88.

## What has not changed

Two things are worth holding on to:

1. **The game is illegal in most of India** and has been throughout its history. See [is matka legal in India?]({{ '/learn/is-matka-legal-in-india.html' | relative_url }})
2. **The payout was always lower than the odds.** The organisers, not the players, made money. See [why number betting always loses]({{ '/learn/why-number-betting-loses.html' | relative_url }}).
