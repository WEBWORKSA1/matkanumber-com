## The feeling

A digit has not come up for twenty draws. Surely it is due? This belief, that a random process will "balance out" soon, is the gambler's fallacy. It feels like common sense, and it has emptied more pockets than any other idea in gambling.

## Why it is wrong

A fair pot, coin or die has **no memory**. Each draw starts fresh. If the chance of a 7 is 1 in 10, it is 1 in 10 after a 7, after no 7 for a month, and after three 7s in a row.

The confusion comes from mixing up two different questions:

- *Before any flips:* what is the chance of six heads in a row? Small: 1 in 64.
- *After five heads have already happened:* what is the chance the next is heads? Exactly 1 in 2.

The five heads are in the past. Their probability is now 100%, because they happened.

## "But it evens out in the long run"

It does, though not by correcting itself. Suppose a coin starts with 10 heads in a row. Flip it 10,000 more times and you would expect about 5,000 more heads and 5,000 more tails. The total is roughly 5,010 to 5,000. The early lead has not been cancelled. It has been **diluted** until it no longer matters. That is the law of large numbers, and it needs no memory at all.

## Hot numbers are the same mistake in reverse

Some people chase numbers that have appeared often lately. In a fair draw, a "hot" number is exactly as likely as a "cold" one. Both beliefs read a pattern into noise.

## See it yourself

Open the [coin flip]({{ '/tools/coin-flip.html' | relative_url }}) and flip 100 times. Watch for streaks and notice how strongly you expect the streak to break. Then remember that the coin does not share the feeling.

## Why our brains do this

People are excellent pattern-spotters, and real life often does have memory: a bus that has not come for a while really is more likely to arrive soon. Random draws are one of the few places where that instinct fails completely, and gambling operators profit from the gap.
